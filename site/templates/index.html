Rust · zero I/O · no async · no clock

The arithmetic between a mobile network and a billing system.

mediation normalises, validates, deduplicates and correlates telecom usage records — and proves it did not lose any. It computes usage, not money: octets, seconds and event counts, attributed to a charged party, which a rating layer then prices.

A pipeline moves records. A mediation layer can prove it did not lose any.

carried_in + ingested == billable + suppressed + duplicate + rejected + held

That equation is a method — BatchReconciliation::balances() — and the crate is built so it holds by construction. A batch that fails it has unknown completeness, and billing over unknown completeness invoices an amount nobody can prove is right.

Four things it refuses to get wrong

Time is local, plus an offset

A charging record carries a local wall clock and a numeric offset counting quarter-hours (3GPP TS 32.298). Reading it as UTC books every evening event of a UTC+X network onto the wrong day, and the offset varies per record, so no constant fixes it downstream.

How time works →

Quantities are integers

A data volume is a count. Above 253 octets — about 9 PB, which a national monthly aggregate crosses — an f64 can no longer represent consecutive integers. No value here is ever routed through a float, and CI greps for one.

Octets, not megabytes →

Loss leaves one trace

A lost record file leaves nothing in the surviving data — no short total, no complaint. The only evidence is a hole in a per-source sequence number, and the trap is the wrap at 99999 → 00001. RFC 1982 arithmetic, exact hole counts, and the source waits until it can account for itself.

Completeness →

Every input is untrusted

Records arrive over a network from a partner's equipment. No parser and no decoder may panic on any input, anything sized by the input is bounded, and nothing is silently skipped. A property suite hammers all of them on every build.

Design constraints →

One pass over a batch, in one call

Every piece works on its own. process is the assembly, and it exists because the order of the pieces is itself a decision that is easy to get wrong quietly — deduplicating after rejecting reports one fault twice; holding a partial run after counting it billable double-bills the session when its successor arrives.

Deduplication and gap detection are inherently stateful, so ProcessState is caller-owned and passed in. That keeps process a pure mapping from (batch, config, as_of, state) to (outcome, state′) — which is what makes a replay reproducible in 2026 and in 2031.

The order, and why it is that one

What it is, and what it is not

In scope

  • Local-time-plus-offset arithmetic, DST-aware, quarter-hour offsets
  • Validated identifiers — IMSI, MSISDN, IMEI, ICCID, SUPI, GPSI, PEI, SUCI, PLMN, TADIG
  • Natural-key deduplication over a bounded window
  • Wrap-safe sequence-gap detection, per source
  • Partial-record reassembly and cross-node correlation
  • Wire codecs: BER framing, TBCD/BCD, ISDN-AddressString, PLMN identity, the full ULI element
  • A numbered rule catalogue, a release gate and a conservation law
  • Roaming batch reconciliation, EU regulatory arithmetic, fraud evidence

Out of scope, deliberately

  • Collection — GTP′, SFTP, Kafka and Diameter peers are I/O
  • Rating — no currency, tariff, bundle, discount or balance type exists here
  • Online charging — a live, stateful conversation with a CHF
  • Orchestration — no scheduler, no retry, no queue, no store
  • A rules GUI — rules are compiled functions with a cited clause, not a scripting runtime

Parsing is not I/O: decoding a record from bytes you already hold is a pure function, so codecs are in scope and transports are not.

Rules are code, not configuration

Mediation is a mature product category, and the platforms that own it are configurable ones: a workflow canvas, a scripting runtime, a rules repository. Their correctness is the correctness of the scripts a systems integrator wrote in them — untyped, untested, and undocumented as to which clause of which standard they implement. Ten years later nobody can say why a field is truncated at eight characters.

This library takes the opposite bet. Every rule is a compiled Rust function that cites its clause at the call site and has a test derived from a published example; behaviour changes only in a semver release; and the correctness claims are proptests you can run.
